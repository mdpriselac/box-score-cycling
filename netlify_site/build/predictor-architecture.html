Same field, seven opinions

How the heads disagree with each other

Every head saw identical inputs on 3 July — the same 184 riders, the same form, the same course profiles. Whatever differs between their outputs is pure architecture: what each head was trained to predict, over which population, with which loss. The Tour runs give us seven parallel experiments, and for GC the devigged book acts as an outside referee.

Finding 1 — nobody here is as sure as the market

"Effective contenders" = exp(entropy) of a win book: how many riders meaningfully share it. Top-3 % = probability mass on the three favorites.

The headline surprise: the two heads' famous disagreement mostly isn't about shape. Force the cascade's quotes into a coherent book (they sum to 1.65 on GC — it's a per-rider conditional head, not a book) and it spreads the race across ~11 effective contenders, almost exactly like LambdaRank. The bookmaker spreads it across 4.4. The cascade's per-rider quote on Pogačar (62.7%) matches the book's 63.0% — but it achieves that while also quoting everyone else generously, which a coherent book can't do. So the deep systematic pattern is not "cascade sharp, LR flat"; it's both heads hedge harder than the market, and the cascade hides it in an inflated book.

Every head makes the same two transfers, in the same direction: out of the top two favorites (book 78.6%, ours ~47–54%) and into the 10–50 band and the extreme tail. The middle-band excess is mostly the information gap — Evenepoel and Lipowitz live there, and our heads don't know what the market knows. The tail excess (11% vs 4.7% on 200/1+ shots) is the models' trained caution: three-week races have delivered enough chaos in the training data that no head will zero out 30 riders the way a bookmaker will. Note how uniform the three columns are — this is a property of the stack, not of any one head.

Score it across the whole market and the split is clean: among favorites and contenders (odds ≤ 50) the book sides with the cascade 5 times out of 6; among longshots it sides with LambdaRank 17 times out of 34. Cascade and LambdaRank order the field near-identically everywhere (0.91–0.96) — their disagreement is magnitude, never ranking. The interesting column is the middle one: the bucket head and the time-gap head agree almost perfectly on mountain days and time trials (0.87–0.96) and decouple completely on sprint days (0.23–0.30). That's physics, not a bug: in a bunch sprint the entire peloton records the same finishing time, so "seconds behind the winner" stops encoding finishing order — the stopwatch goes blind exactly when the sorter still sees fine. It's the clearest demonstration in the data that the heads measure different physical quantities, which is why stacking them as features works. Elo, meanwhile, is the least redundant signal in the stack: rank agreement with the cascade of only 0.37–0.56, highest on the sprint-and-punchy profiles where finishes are literally contested duels (which is what Elo counts) and lowest on time trials, where nothing is. Low redundancy is exactly why it earned its place as a feature.

Every other head redistributes ~1.0 of win probability between riders. The DNF head's output moves with the scenario itself.

Single stages barely register (0.2–0.6% mean — you rarely abandon on the day itself), the time trial is the safest of all, and the GC scenario jumps to 7.3% mean with individual riders above 20% — because finishing a three-week race is a genuinely different proposition than finishing a Tuesday. This is why DNF multiplication lives at the pricing layer: it's a per-scenario survival discount, orthogonal to the who-beats-whom question every other head answers.

The systematic patterns, in one list

  1. Both probability heads hedge harder than the market. ~11 effective GC contenders for us, 4.4 for the book. The cascade's sharp top quote coexists with an inflated book (sum 1.65); as a coherent book it's as flat as LambdaRank.
  2. The mass we shave off favorites goes two places: the 10–50 odds band (mostly the Evenepoel/Lipowitz information gap) and the extreme tail (trained three-week-chaos caution the book doesn't share).
  3. The market endorses the two-head split: cascade closer to the book at the top (5/6), LambdaRank closer in the tail (17/34) — the corrector-plus-blend pricing layer already implements exactly this.
  4. Ordering is consensus, magnitude is contested. Cascade~LR rank agreement is 0.92+ in every scenario; every visible disagreement is about confidence, not ranking.
  5. The time-gap head goes blind on sprint days (rank agreement with the bucket head: ~0.95 in the mountains, ~0.25 in sprints) — same-time bunch finishes carry no gap signal. Its stack-feature value is concentrated on selective terrain.
  6. DNF is a level, not an allocation: ~0.4% on a stage, 7.3% over three weeks — the only head whose scenario response is about survival rather than hierarchy.

Model vs market

Our numbers against the book

Odds captured 3 July 2026 (the day before the Grand Départ) from the Kambi feed that powers Unibet — the same numbers shown on the consumer site, taken in one API snapshot and archived. Bookmaker odds carry a margin (this win market sums to 22% over a fair book), so every "Book %" below is devigged: rescaled so the market's probabilities sum to what a fair book would.

Which of our heads to compare: the LambdaRank column is our calibrated book — that's the honest apples-to-apples line. The cascade is our winner-picker, sharp at the top but not book-normalized. One known artifact matters here: LambdaRank's per-race softmax refuses extreme favorites, so its Pogačar number is a floor, not an opinion — at the very top of the market, the cascade is our real quote.

Three stories in this table

Where we agree

The two systems shake hands at the very top.

Book devig on Pogačar: 63.0%. Our cascade: 62.7%. Two completely different machines — a global betting market and a LightGBM stack trained on result sheets — land within a point of each other on the biggest question. Seixas is the same story in miniature: the book makes the 19-year-old third favorite (5.5%), and our heads have him 6–10%. Del Toro, Ayuso, Johannessen, Carapaz: all within ~1pp of the book. When we agree this closely, both systems are probably right, and there is no bet.

What the books see that we don't

The giant gaps are information, not value.

Evenepoel: book 2.4%, our blend 22.7%. Lipowitz: book 2.0%, blend 16.2%. And in the "winner without Pogačar" market the book gives Vingegaard 47.9% of the non-Pogačar race where we give him 19%. Read together, the market is telling one coherent story — it believes only one rider genuinely challenges Pogačar, and that Evenepoel and Lipowitz will not contest GC at full strength. One important self-correction on Vingegaard specifically: a backtest run after this snapshot found the model systematically underprices riders who finished a grand tour within ~45 days (they win GC at ~1.6× our quote) — Vingegaard won the Giro 33 days ago, so much of his gap is our measured bias, not the book's private information. Our database runs to 21 June but contains results, not news: no crash reports, no leadership announcements, no route knowledge — and Evenepoel has not raced since 26 April, a fact our model can only see as stale form while the market knows the reason. A 10× gap on a headline rider means the market knows a fact we don't — the move is to find the fact, not to bet against it.

Where we'd explore for edges

Small, calibrated disagreements — not the screaming gaps.

The credible candidates are where our calibrated head sits modestly above the devigged book with no obvious news story: Ayuso +2.1pp and del Toro +2.0pp — notably the pair who just finished 3rd and 1st at the Dauphiné, a result the model has digested and this odds snapshot predates — plus Seixas (+0.7pp) and Pidcock fractionally. Structurally, the softer targets are the secondary markets — top-6 and top-10 carry ~41% margins (vs 22% on the win market), and place probabilities are exactly what our DNF-adjusted, calibrated stack does best. That's where a retail book is most likely to be lazy, and where our honest prices have teeth.

What happens next

This snapshot is archived (raw API payloads + parsed markets + margin diagnostics) as the first entry of a going-forward odds archive, alongside our own logged predictions. After Paris, the two get settled against results — per-market log loss for us and for the devigged book, the same yardstick our experiment gate uses. That, repeated over races, is the only honest answer to "can this model beat the books" — one Tour is a single sample. Until then: the model's job this month is to be well-calibrated; the market's job is to know things. The interesting work lives in the gap.

Reading notes

Why the boards look different per archetype

Only two things change between scenarios: the course profile fed to the models (the bars on each card) and the stage-context the DNF head sees. The startlist, every rider's form, Elo, and team context stay identical — so the differences between boards are purely the model's view of rider-course fit. The win books also behave differently by design: the cascade's book is unconstrained (it's a per-rider conditional head), widest on open sprint days and most concentrated when one rider dominates a profile.

The friendly tour

What is this thing?

It's a computer program that has studied ten years of professional bike-race results — over 660,000 of them — and uses what it learned to estimate each rider's chances before a race: chance of winning, of finishing on the podium, of making the top 5, and so on.

Think of it like a weather forecast, but for bike races. A forecast doesn't tell you it will definitely rain; it says "70% chance of rain," and a good forecaster is right about 7 times out of 10 when they say that. This system aims for the same thing: percentages you can actually trust, race after race.

1
Line them up

Put all ~180 starters in expected finishing order.

2
Zoom in on the favorites

Take the top 15 and split the winning chances between them.

3
Double-check the math

A second, more cautious estimate keeps the percentages honest.

4
Reality check

Factor in the chance a rider crashes out or quits before the finish.

The team behind the forecast

A panel of specialists, one prediction

Under the hood it isn't one big brain — it's a panel of specialists, each trained to answer one question really well. Like a good cycling team, each has a job:

The Sorter bucket model

"Roughly where does each rider finish?"

Looks at every starter's history and lines the whole field up in expected finishing order — like a talent scout ranking the startlist from 1 to 180. Whoever lands in its top 15 is treated as a genuine favorite by everyone else on the panel.

The Stopwatch time-gap model

"OK, but by how much?"

Two riders can both "lose" a race — one by 2 seconds in a photo finish, the other rolling in 12 minutes later. The Stopwatch predicts the actual time behind the winner, which tells the panel how close the race really was for each rider.

The Bold Tipster contender model

"Of the favorites, who actually wins?"

Ignores everyone except the ~15 genuine favorites and divides the winning chances between them. Opinionated and sharp — this is the specialist that's best at actually naming the winner, and its numbers are the headline percentages in the app.

The Careful Accountant LambdaRank model

"Are those percentages actually fair?"

Prices every rider, not just favorites, and its specialty is percentages that mean what they say: across all the riders it gives "10%" to, almost exactly 1 in 10 win. Less flashy than the Tipster, but this is who you'd trust when comparing against a bookmaker.

The Crash Forecaster DNF model

"Will they even reach the finish?"

About 1 rider in 10 doesn't finish a pro race — crashes, illness, exhaustion, or quitting to save energy for another day. This specialist estimates that risk for each rider, so the other predictions can be adjusted for it.

The Race Director GC outlook model

"Halfway through a 3-week race — now who wins?"

Only wakes up during multi-day races like the Tour de France. Once real stages have been ridden, it uses the actual standings — who's leading, by how many seconds — instead of pretending the race hasn't started yet.

The Duel Judge contested Elo rating

"When it came down to a real fight, who won?"

Keeps a chess-style rating for every rider, but only counts moments that were genuinely contested — a sprint decided at the line, one group distancing another. Beating 50 riders who'd already given up doesn't move your rating; outkicking the world champion does.

The Fairness Adjuster win corrector

"Is the Tipster selling winners short?"

Over thousands of races we measured that the Bold Tipster is slightly too shy about winners' chances. This is a single, carefully measured nudge that corrects for it — like knowing your bathroom scale reads a pound heavy and adjusting accordingly.

The Casting Director day-menu ranker · new

"Given the kind of day this is, who belongs in the conversation?"

The newest hire, added after the Tour. Tell it what kind of stage tomorrow is — a mountain-escape day, a split sprint — and it re-picks the shortlist of favorites using ten years of "who actually shows up on days like this." It only decides who's on the shortlist and in what order; it never touches anyone's price.

Worked example

Follow one rider through the machine

Say it's the morning of a big one-day race, and we follow a star sprinter — call him Rider X — through the whole pipeline:

Rider X's morning, step by step

  1. The Sorter looks at his results, his recent form, and how well the course suits a sprinter, and ranks him 2nd of 180 starters. He's officially a favorite.
  2. The Bold Tipster weighs him against the other 14 favorites and says: 24% to win, 55% to finish on the podium. The Fairness Adjuster nudges the win number up to 25%.
  3. The Careful Accountant is more conservative: 19% to win.
  4. The app blends the two, leaning toward the Accountant (whose percentages are more trustworthy): about 20%.
  5. The Crash Forecaster gives him a 4% chance of not finishing. You can't stand on a podium if you're in the ambulance — so his honest podium chance becomes 55% × 96% ≈ 53%.

And here's the important part: the app shows you every one of those numbers, not just the final one. If you want the bold pick, it's there. If you want the cautious price, it's there too. Nothing gets hidden inside the machine.

A fair question

Why show two different win percentages?

Because they're good at different things — like two friends you'd ask about the race.

One friend is bold: fantastic at calling the winner, but when he says "40% chance," he's often overexcited. The other is careful: her percentages are almost eerily reliable, but she spreads her confidence so evenly that she rarely pounds the table for anyone.

You wouldn't average your friends into one bland opinion and throw away the originals. So the app shows both, plus a blend — and when the two friends disagree strongly about a rider (more than 2 percentage points on the win chance), it highlights that rider in yellow. Big disagreement is useful information: it means "look closer at this one."

New for the Vuelta 2026

Telling the machine what kind of day it is

The Tour de France was a month-long exam, and the machine mostly passed: bunch sprints and summit finishes were priced well. But it also exposed the one place the machine is structurally weak — the in-between stages, where a breakaway stays away or a late attacker wins from a small group. On those days the pre-race favorites list kept featuring the usual stars, while the actual winner came from a type of rider the boards had buried.

The fix is not new percentages. We tested that thoroughly, and every version of "let the day type change the prices" made the predictions worse. What works is narrower and safer: a menu of eleven day types — mountain-escape day, hard puncheur day, split sprint, survivor's gallop, and so on — each defined by who historically contests the front on days like it, not by the shape of the course.

Each race morning, a human reads the stage and keys it to a menu item (or a weighted mix of two — "60% mountain escape, 40% hard puncheur"). The Casting Director then re-picks the shortlist: riders who thrive on that kind of day move up into the favorites conversation, riders who don't slide out of it. Everything else stays exactly as before — the prices still come from the same specialists, untouched. If the key is wrong, the cost is a worse shortlist for a day, never a corrupted price.

In the dress rehearsal we replayed Tour stage 4 — a hilly stage won from a hard late move — with the key "mountain escape day." The keyed shortlist put Mads Pedersen first (he won it), pulled several eventual breakaway riders up from as deep as 88th, and dropped Tadej Pogačar down the list while his win price stayed exactly the same. The Vuelta a España is the live test: every morning's key gets logged before the stage, and every evening it's graded against what actually happened.

Keeping it honest

Can it see the future? How good is it really?

The model learned by replaying ten years of races under one strict rule: when predicting any race from the past, it's only allowed to use what was knowable the day before that race. No peeking at results that hadn't happened yet — the software equivalent of predicting last Sunday's race using only the previous Saturday's newspaper.

How often does it name the actual winner of a one-day race? About 1 time in 4. If that sounds low, consider: a pro race has ~180 riders, crosswinds, crashes, punctures, and team tactics. Even the bookmakers' favorite usually loses. The real goal isn't naming every winner — it's percentages that hold up over a whole season, so that the riders given "20%" really do win about a fifth of the time. That's the standard every one of the seven specialists is graded against.

And one disclaimer: these are model estimates for research and fun — not betting advice.

The 30-second version

One race, one pass through the stack

Paste a race URL into the app and it scrapes the startlist, attaches each rider's history as-of today, and runs the field through a stack of models. Each model answers a different question — who's strongest, by how much, will they finish at all — and a final pricing layer turns those answers into a coherent set of sportsbook-style probabilities.

The main line ranks, gates, and prices. Two side lines — a calibration head and a finishing head — join at the pricing layer rather than being averaged in earlier. The GC outlook head runs beside the stack, not through it; the new day-menu channel can re-decide who passes the top-15 gate on a keyed stage day, but never touches a price.

A one-line mental model: the bucket head ranks the field → the cascade prices the top → the corrector fixes its win bias → the blend borrows LambdaRank's calibration → the DNF adjustment makes the prices honest — and every intermediate number stays visible in the table rather than being swallowed by the final one.

The riders on this team

Seven heads, seven different questions

Every head is a LightGBM model, but each differs on three axes: what it predicts, who it compares (the whole field? only contenders? riders who didn't finish?), and what loss it optimizes. Those choices determine what its number means — and why no single head is enough.

Results are scored on a tier scale: winner → podium → top 5 → top 15 → top 30 → top 50 → finisher, referred to below as buckets.

Bucket head

"Roughly where in the result sheet does this rider land?"

Full field Ordinal regression

The ranking backbone. Predicts a continuous finishing tier for every starter and sorts the field. It is also the gatekeeper: its top 15 defines who counts as a contender everywhere downstream.

Time-gap head

"How far behind the winner, in seconds?"

Full field Regression

Same ordering intuition as the bucket head, but in physical units — a 2-second gap and a 12-minute gap can be the same tier, and this head knows the difference. That magnitude signal is why its prediction is also fed back into the cascade as a feature.

Contender cascade

"Among the genuine contenders, how do the top placings split?"

Top-15 finishers 5-class softmax

The headline pricing head. Trained only on gated contenders, with the bucket head's own opinion as an input, it is sharp at separating winner from podium from top 5. Its probabilities are conditional — on being a contender, and on finishing. Drives the displayed Win / Podium / Top 5 / Top 15 % and the odds.

LambdaRank head

"Over the entire startlist, what's one coherent win book?"

Full field Ranking loss

Trained with a learning-to-rank objective and converted to probabilities by a per-race softmax with a fitted temperature. It never beat the cascade at picking winners — but its probabilities are almost perfectly calibrated, which is exactly what you want when comparing against a bookmaker's line.

DNF head

"Will they finish at all?"

All starters Binary classifier

The only head whose training population includes riders who abandoned. Every other probability is implicitly "given that they finish" — this head supplies the missing factor (AUC ≈ 0.89). Applied only at the pricing layer; mixing it into the finish-conditioned heads was tested and made things worse.

GC outlook head

"Given the standings after stage k, who wins the overall?"

Mid-race state Bucket + cascade

A state-aware re-run of the main architecture for stage races: it sees each rider's current GC time, rank, and gap. Mid-race it beats simply carrying pre-race predictions forward by ~18 points of winner accuracy. Deliberately shown as its own panel, never merged into the pre-race table.

Contested Elo

"How strong is this rider when a result was actually disputed?"

All riders Rating system

Not a model head but a measurement: an Elo rating built only from informative comparisons — contested sprints within the winner's group, splits between adjacent groups. It enters the models as a feature; the app also shows it raw, with the number of comparisons behind it.

Win corrector

"Is the cascade systematically shy about winners?"

Contender rows Logit shift

A single fitted parameter, not a model: a logit-scale shift that fixes the cascade's measured win-market under-confidence (validation calibration 0.97 → 1.00 at the last training run). Applied before anything else in the pricing layer.

Team tactics

How the heads combine — two distinct mechanisms

Inside the models: feature stacking

The cascade doesn't work alone. At training time, out-of-fold predictions from the time-gap and LambdaRank heads are added to its feature set, alongside the contested Elo rating and — for one-day races — contest signatures: a forecast of which kind of rider (sprinter, climber, puncheur…) will actually contest the finish, built from the startlist's composition and the race's history. At prediction time the same 17 columns are attached from the live heads, so training and serving see the same world.

After the models: the pricing layer

Three post-processing steps, each book-preserving — they reshape who gets which share of probability without inflating the total implied over the field.

Win corrector

The fitted logit shift is applied to contender win probabilities and the odds are recomputed. Deliberately not renormalized — pinning the book here was the root cause of an earlier calibration bug.

Blend win market

The sharp cascade and the calibrated LambdaRank book are combined into a third number, then rescaled so the field's total win probability still matches the cascade's book:

blend = 0.2 × cascade + 0.8 × LambdaRank  →  renormalized to the cascade book

An overlay that borrows LambdaRank's calibration without changing the total vig.

DNF adjustment — honest prices

Every market is multiplied by the probability of finishing:

P(podium) = P(podium | finishes) × P(finishes)

Podium / top-5 / top-15 shrink directly (you can't podium if you don't finish); the win market is renormalized within the race so the total win book is preserved. The result is the price you'd actually want to compare against a bookmaker, whose "did not finish" is just a losing ticket.

Where heads deliberately stay separate

The cascade's Win % and LambdaRank's Win % are shown side by side, and any rider where they disagree by 2+ percentage points is highlighted. That disagreement is a feature, not a bug — the two heads carry genuinely different signal, and a large gap is the app telling you to look closer.

Win % — the cascade

Use it to pick winners. Sharpest at separating the very best riders from each other; conditioned on contending and finishing.

LR Win % — LambdaRank

Use it to compare against a book. Near-perfectly calibrated across the whole field; the honest baseline for value hunting.

Likewise, the GC outlook stays a separate panel, the Elo rating stays a raw column, and every run — both heads' probabilities, the blend, DNF, adjusted prices — is logged to a prediction database for long-term comparison against real results and real odds.

New — August 2026

The day-type retrieval channel

The Tour exposed the stack's one structural hole: transition stages decided by a surviving break or a hard late move. The boards priced sprints and summit finishes well, but on those in-between days the winner repeatedly came from a rider class the pre-race top 15 had buried. A month of gated experiments produced a fix — and, just as importantly, established what the fix is not allowed to be.

The menu

Eleven day types, defined in rider-composition space — who contests the front ~15 (sprinters, climbers, puncheurs, rouleurs, break specialists…), not what the profile looks like. Three are routed mechanically (individual time trial; full bunch sprint; GC day, where the overall contenders fight for the win). The other eight are analog day types learned from a decade of stages, organized in two sharply distinct families: escape/climb (mountain escape → hard puncheur → rouleur power group) and power-sprint (survivor's gallop → split sprint → fast-finisher group). Within a family the items are graded neighbors; across families they barely overlap. The menu is a frozen artifact — assigning a day is a stored-parameter lookup, never a refit.

The ranker

A retrained variant of the bucket head with 14 extra columns: the keyed day type's canonical composition, plus per-rider kernel affinities — how often the rider shows up, and how they finish, on historically similar days (exposure, attendance, top-3 rate, mean finish), computed strictly as-of race morning from 642K historical rider-rides with a two-year half-life. It engages only when a stage is keyed.

What it may and may not do

Every stronger use of this signal was tested and failed honestly, which is why the channel is retrieval-only:

DesignVerdictEvidence
Day-type features inside the pricing headsRejected+0.011 log loss vs the market benchmark, worse on 5 of 5 folds — a global tax paid even by races the channel never touches
Conditional boards served as pricesRejected+0.016 even with perfect keys. The signal is top-15-grade: day-type affinity says who shows up at the front, not who wins the kick
Retrieval only — pool membership and board orderShippedPricing neutral (+0.0005); with judgment-grade keys, top-15 pool coverage +2.45pp and mean winner rank −1.84 on the hard residual days, 5 of 5 folds

The structural guarantee follows from the wiring, not from good intentions: the keyed ranker can only change who passes the top-15 gate and in what order. Riders it promotes are priced by the same production contender cascade, corrector, blend, and DNF adjustment as anyone else. A wrong key costs pool composition for a day; it cannot corrupt a price.

Serving it at the Vuelta

Each race morning, a human keys the stage to a menu item or a weighted mix ("mountain escape 0.6 / hard puncheur 0.4" — the mix happens at the ranking-score level, never by averaging prices). Stage pages show the key as a keyed: chip; unkeyed stages serve the plain production board; one-day races key themselves from their own historical signature. In the dress rehearsal — Tour stage 4 replayed under a mountain-escape key — the keyed board put Mads Pedersen first (he won), promoted the eventual break riders from as deep as 88th into the pool, and dropped Pogačar down the order with his price untouched.

The Vuelta a España 2026 is the channel's prospective evaluation: every morning's key is archived before the stage, every evening it's scored against an oracle labeler that keys the day from the actual result. That scorecard — not the Tour replay — will decide whether human day-reading is close enough to the ceiling for the channel to stay on by default.

The engine room

What it's all built on

A weekly pipeline scrapes results from FirstCycling, migrates them into SQLite, and precomputes ~1.1M point-in-time rider snapshots back to 2010 — every feature strictly "as of the day before the race," so the models never peek at the future. Training covers 2016–2026: about 668K rider-race rows across 5,905 competitions at the WorldTour, ProSeries, and .1 levels. The full suite was last retrained on 2026-08-09, on a corpus current through that day — including the complete Tour de France 2026 and a repaired 2025 late-season block (a source-site markup change had silently un-dated 35 races, Vuelta 2025 among them; they were caught, re-scraped, and re-migrated before this training run).

Each rider carries 164 features into a prediction — long-run quality percentiles, recent-form trajectories (recomputed live as of prediction day), team context, race-profile fit against the course's gradients — plus 17 stacked columns (head outputs, contested Elo, contest signatures), for 181 into the flagship ranking head; the day-menu variant adds 14 more. Every prediction run is persisted, so the system continuously builds the dataset needed to judge itself against real books.
