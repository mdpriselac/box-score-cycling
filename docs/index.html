How the Race Predictor Works

A plain-language tour: from raw result sheets, to what the system learns about races and riders, to the predictions it makes — and how to read them. No machine-learning background needed.

This system predicts professional road-cycling results: who is likely to win, make the podium, or place in the top 5/15 of any World Tour, Pro Series, or .1-level race — expressed as probabilities and sportsbook-style odds. It learns from a decade of race results scraped from FirstCycling, and it makes predictions for upcoming races from nothing more than the startlist and the race's own history.

1. Everything starts with the result sheet

Almost everything the system knows comes from one humble document: the finishing sheet of past races — each rider's name, team, finishing position, and time gap to the winner. We have those sheets for every race back to 2010. The trick is that a result sheet can be read three different ways, and each reading teaches us something different:

MILANO–SANREMO — RESULT
1. M. van der Poel6:21:14
2. T. Pogačar+ 0:00
3. F. Ganna+ 0:00
4. M. Matthews+ 0:21
5. T. Pidcock+ 0:21
…
97. J. Doe+ 12:44
— R. SmithDNF
→

Read the gaps → what kind of race was it?

If 80 riders finish together, it was a sprint. If the sheet shatters into small groups minutes apart, it was a mountain day. The pattern of time gaps is a fingerprint of the course — no GPS or elevation data needed.

Read the names → how good is each rider, and at what?

Every sheet adds one line to each rider's career ledger: how they fare in sprints vs. mountains, at World Tour vs. smaller races, whether their form is rising or fading right now.

Read the order up front → who beat whom when it counted?

The first ten riders of the winning group genuinely raced each other — that finishing order is a real head-to-head result, like a chess game. Deep in the bunch, order is meaningless and we ignore it. Abandons (DNF) get their own ledger too.

That third reading — and the abandon ledger — are the newest additions (June 2026). They recover information the older system threw away: it used to treat "8th place" and "85th place, rolled in with the gruppetto" with the same seriousness.

2. Two libraries: one about races, one about riders

From thousands of sheets the system builds two bodies of knowledge. Both are rebuilt automatically every week as new results arrive.

The race library — every race's character

"What kind of bike race is this, really?"

  • Race profile scores. Each past race gets six scores summing to 1 — sprint, mountain, hilly, rolling, breakaway, time-trial — computed purely from how its finishing gaps were distributed. We measure the gap pattern 19 times (front 10% of the field, front 15%, … whole field) and watch how it changes; that curve separates a bunch sprint from a summit finish remarkably well.
  • Front-group depth. How much of the field finished in the winner's group — the single best "how selective was it?" number on the sheet.
  • Selection signatures next retrain. Not just how many contested the finale but who: what mix of sprinters / climbers / puncheurs filled the top 10. It turns out the kind of rider who contests a given race is about twice as stable year-over-year as the size of the front group — Sanremo keeps selecting the same kind of finisher even when the race plays out differently.
  • Forecasting a future race's character. An upcoming edition has no result sheet yet, so the system looks at the same race's past editions plus who actually showed up this year. A startlist stacked with sprinters pulls the forecast toward a sprint; history says how much this course usually resists that.

The rider library — every rider's story so far

"Who is this rider, as of this morning?"

  • The career ledger. Average results split by race type (one-day / stage / GC), level (WT / Pro / .1), and terrain — each race counts toward the terrains it actually featured.
  • Form & trajectory live. Beyond "last 5 races": an exponentially-weighted recent-percentile curve (last month counts much more than three months ago), season-over-season trajectory, distance from career peak. Added June 2026 — it fixed a long-standing bias where fading veterans were rated on their glory years. These features are also re-aged to race morning: a rider idle for six weeks is predicted as rusty today, not as fresh as their last start.
  • Contested-duel rating next retrain. A chess-style Elo rating built only from finishes where riders genuinely raced each other (front of the winning group, group-vs-group). Currently rates Pogačar #1 — and spots hot neo-pros the career averages haven't caught up with.
  • Finishing risk next retrain. A dedicated abandon model: 1.26M start records (10.6% DNFs) teach it who tends not to finish — crash-prone riders, riders deep into a grand tour, classics crashers.
  • Team context. Strength and depth of the rider's squad at this race, leadership (bib number 1), protected-rider status.

The golden rule: frozen in time

Every fact about a rider is computed as of the morning of the race being predicted — never using anything that happened later. When the system trains on a 2021 race, the riders look exactly as they looked in 2021. This "no peeking" rule is enforced everywhere: career stats, form, Elo ratings, abandon history, even mid-race standings. It's why accuracy measured on past races is an honest estimate of accuracy on next week's.

3. From libraries to predictions

For an upcoming race, the system pulls each starter's rider-library entry (re-aged to today), the race-library fingerprint (from reference editions you pick — typically the last few years of the same race), and facts about the field itself. That bundle — about 164 facts per rider — goes to a set of specialist models. Each model answers one question:

QuestionModelWhat it produces
Who finishes ahead of whom? Ranking model live A score for every starter, sorted into the predicted finishing order. This drives the main table's rank column.
Will they win? Podium? Top 5? Top 15? Contender model live Probabilities for the riders who plausibly matter (top ~15 by the ranking model). Sharpest at naming the winner — the "scout".
What's a fair price on each rider? LambdaRank model live Win/podium probabilities for the whole field, tuned so the percentages are honest (10% chances really happen ~1 time in 10) — the "bookmaker". Shown side-by-side with the scout; disagreements ≥ 2 points are highlighted.
How far behind the winner? Time-gap model live Expected seconds behind the winner for each finisher.
Mid-race: who wins the GC from here? GC outlook model live During a stage race, re-forecasts the final general classification from the live situation: current standings, gaps, who's looked strong, stages left, rest days. Uses only the race situation — it knows a 4-minute lead with two flat stages left is nearly decisive regardless of who holds it.
Will they even finish? Abandon (DNF) model next retrain Probability each rider finishes at all. Used to mark down prices on fragile riders: a podium price becomes P(podium if they finish) × P(they finish).

Under the hood all of these are gradient-boosted decision-tree models (LightGBM) — the same family that wins most tabular-data competitions — trained on the 643K historical rider-race results. The newest upgrade (June 2026) also lets the main models see each other's outputs: the contender model now receives the time-gap and bookmaker estimates, the duel ratings, and the selection signatures as additional inputs.

4. How we keep ourselves honest

Probability quality is the bar

Since June 2026, an upgrade ships only if it improves the quality of the prices — measured across the win, podium, top-5 and top-15 markets for every rider, not just the ones who happened to finish well. "Did we name the winner?" is still reported, but it's a diagnostic, not the gate.

Honest test design

Candidate upgrades are tested on five full held-out years the models never saw, train-on-past / test-on-future only. Most ideas fail this bar and are documented as failures — the experiment log records every dead end.

Calibration, not bravado

The bookmaker model's stated percentages match reality almost exactly (when it says 10%, it happens 9.9–10.2% of the time). Where a model runs hot or cold — e.g. early-race GC win odds — the docs say so.

Every prediction is logged

Each prediction run is written to a permanent log with both models' probabilities, so we can audit later how the system performed against the books — with no ability to quietly forget bad calls.
