Reading the Predictions

What every number in the prediction table means, which model produced it, how much to trust it, and how the numbers become betting-style odds. Companion to How It Works.

Rank predicted_rank

The predicted finishing order, from a score that blends the ranking model's expected finishing bucket with the predicted time gap. Treat the top of the list as meaningful and the middle as fuzzy: the difference between rank 2 and rank 5 is real signal; the difference between rank 38 and rank 45 is noise.

Expected Bucket expected_bucket

The ranking model's raw output on a 0–7 scale, where 0 = winner, 1 = podium, 2 = top 5, 3 = top 15, 4 = top 30, 5 = top 50, 6 = finisher outside the top 50. A value of 1.6 reads as "between a podium and a top-5 type ride". Lower is better. It's the sort key behind the rank column — useful for seeing how close neighbouring riders really are.

Win / Podium / Top 5 / Top 15 % multiclass cascade

The scout's probabilities (see §2). These are cumulative: "Podium %" means top-3 including winning, "Top 15 %" includes all of the above. Riders outside the plausible-contender pool get small probabilities by construction.

LR Win / LR Podium % LambdaRank head

The bookmaker's view of the same two questions (see §2). These are the calibrated, price-grade numbers — the ones to compare against actual sportsbook odds.

Δ Win pp |scout − bookmaker|

The gap between the two win estimates, in percentage points. Rows at ≥ 2.0 points are highlighted yellow. A big gap isn't an error — it means the two models read the race differently, and that's worth a human look: the scout leans on "who wins races like this", the bookmaker on the whole field's pecking order.

Time Gap (s) time-gap model

Expected seconds behind the winner, for riders expected to finish. Predictions are made in log-space (the model is much surer about "2 minutes vs 10 minutes" than "12:10 vs 12:40"), so read large values as orders of magnitude, not stopwatch times. Capped at one hour in training.

Contender flag is_contender

Marks the ~15 riders per race the scout actually evaluated in detail. Everyone else's win/podium numbers come from the field-level fallback and are deliberately conservative.

2. Why two probability models?

Naming winners and pricing the field are different jobs, and optimizing one hurts the other. So the system runs both and shows them side by side:

The Scout — contender cascade

"Who wins races like this one?"
  • First narrows the field to the ~15 plausible contenders, then studies only them with a dedicated model.
  • Sharpest at the very top: its #1 pick wins about 1 race in 4 — in fields of 120–180 riders.
  • Probabilities are good but run slightly bold: it will say 28% where the fair number is closer to 25%.
  • Use it for: who to watch, talking points, "who's the danger man".

The Bookmaker — LambdaRank head

"What's the fair price on every rider?"
  • Scores the entire startlist in one pass and converts scores to probabilities that sum correctly across the race.
  • Honest percentages: across thousands of held-out predictions its stated chances match observed frequencies within about 1% relative (calibration ratio ≈ 1.01).
  • Slightly less sharp at naming the single winner than the scout.
  • Use it for: comparing against sportsbook odds, expected value, anything where the number itself matters.
Rule of thumb: read the order from the scout, read the prices from the bookmaker, and pay attention wherever they disagree by more than 2 points — that's where the race has a genuine open question.

3. Calibration in one minute

A model is calibrated if, whenever it says "X%", the thing happens X% of the time. Calibration is measured on years of races the models never trained on. The calibration ratio below is predicted ÷ actual — 1.00 is perfect, above 1.00 means overconfident (says 10%, happens less often), below means shy.

4. From probabilities to odds

The odds expander shows every probability as a price, in three formats. These are fair-value odds — straight conversions with no bookmaker margin baked in. If a real book offers longer odds than the fair price, the bet has positive expected value if you trust the model's number (use the bookmaker column for that reason).

5. The GC Outlook (stage races only)

What it is

When you predict the general classification of a stage race already underway, a separate "GC Outlook" section appears. It re-forecasts the final GC from the live race situation: current standings and time gaps, who has looked strong on the road, how much racing is left, attrition, rest days. It deliberately ignores reputations — a 4-minute lead with two flat stages left is nearly decisive whether the leader is a superstar or a surprise.

Why it exists: pre-race models keep predicting from reputation even when the road has already decided otherwise. Mid-race, the outlook model names the eventual GC winner dramatically more often than carrying forward pre-race picks (+18 percentage points in early-race tests, improving as the race goes on).

How to read it: trust the ordering and the cliff-edges (who is genuinely still in contention). Early in a race its win percentages run shy (calibration ≈ 0.83) — the favourite's true chance is usually a bit higher than shown. By the final stages the numbers tighten up.

6. Finishing risk — the abandon model next retrain

What's changing

Today's probabilities largely assume the rider completes the race. A dedicated abandon model (trained on 1.26M start records, of which 10.6% ended in a DNF) is validated and being wired into the pricing layer. Once live, displayed prices become:

P(podium) = P(podium given they finish) × P(they finish)

In backtests this matters most for one-day classics — crash-heavy races where some fast finishers carry real abandon risk — and it changes little for most stage-race favourites. The model separates "did not start" from "did not finish", and knows each rider's abandon history, age, and how deep into a stage race they are.

7. How good is it, really?

  • Winner naming: the top pick wins ~24–25% of races on held-out years (random guessing in a 150-rider field: under 1%; picking the bookmakers' favourite gets roughly 20–25% depending on race type — the system is competitive with the market on its best surfaces and behind on others; the audit log will settle this precisely over time).
  • Price quality: the bookmaker head's probabilities are calibrated to ≈ 1% relative error across win/podium markets, measured on five held-out years.
  • Podium hit rate: the top pick lands on the podium in ~44% of races.
  • Known soft spots: first-year neo-pros with no result history (the duel-rating upgrade helps); chaotic crosswind/crash races; tactics the sheet can't see (team orders, sprint trains failing); 2023 as a test year is mysteriously harder than its neighbours (documented, unexplained).

Every upgrade must clear a five-year backtest gate on market price quality (win, podium, top-5, top-15 — scored for every rider, so overconfident prices on flops are punished). Improvements of even half a percent are rare and treated as wins; most candidate ideas fail and are archived in the experiment log.

8. Sensible use

Do

  • Compare bookmaker-column prices to real books — that's the number built for it.
  • Mine the disagreements. Yellow rows (Δ ≥ 2pp) are the most informative thing on the page.
  • Pick reference races thoughtfully. The race fingerprint comes from the editions you select; a course change makes old editions misleading.
  • Use the GC outlook mid-race — that's exactly when pre-race opinions go stale.
  • Check the contender flag before quoting a longshot's win probability.

Don't

  • Don't read mid-pack ranks literally. Rank 38 vs 45 is noise; buckets and probabilities carry the real information.
  • Don't treat 999 odds as a price — it's a display cap on "essentially zero".
  • Don't expect tactics. The model prices ability and fit, not team orders, weather, or a sprint train misfiring.
  • Don't bet the scout's percentages. It's tuned to find winners, and pays for that with slightly bold probabilities.
  • Don't average the two heads yourself — a validated blend is already part of the next-retrain stack.
